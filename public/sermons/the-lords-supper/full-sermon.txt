1. Introduction: What Is Holy Communion?
Hallelujah! Praise the Lord! Beloved brothers and sisters, and all who are
joining this worship service from different parts of the world, I welcome
you in the precious name of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ. This is the
day the Lord has made. May His Word enlighten us, strengthen our faith,
and lead us into the fullness of His grace. Can somebody say Amen?

Our subject today is Holy Communion, also called the Lord’s Supper and
the Eucharist. We shall examine its biblical meaning and then address a
serious question raised by some critics: Is the Christian Lord’s Supper a
borrowed ceremony from ancient pagan worship, especially the cult of
the Greek god Dionysus?

First, let us understand the terminology. In Urdu Christian usage the
expression “ʿAshāʾ-e Rabbānī” (‫ )عشائے ربانی‬means the Lord’s Supper or the
Lord’s meal. In the New Testament, Paul explicitly speaks of the “Lord’s
supper” in 1 Corinthians 11:20. The word “communion” emphasizes
fellowship or participation; compare the Greek term koinōnia in 1
Corinthians 10:16. “Eucharist” derives from the Greek eucharistia,
thanksgiving, and recalls the thanks Jesus gave over the bread and the
cup. [1]

We are not gathered to perform an empty religious custom. We come to
remember the saving work of Jesus Christ, to confess our dependence
upon Him, to share fellowship with His people, and to proclaim His death
until He comes. If we understand the Scriptures, we shall not be unsettled
by superficial resemblances or unfounded accusations. The Word of God
calls us to examine all things carefully.


2. The Bread of Life and the Difficult Saying
Please open the Gospel according to John, chapter 6. Jesus is teaching in
Capernaum, in the synagogue (John 6:59). The crowd had heard about
bread from heaven and remembered the manna given to Israel in the
wilderness. Jesus took that familiar image and revealed something
greater: He Himself is the bread of life. The manna sustained Israel for a
time; the Son gives life that endures.

In John 6:51, Jesus says that the bread He will give for the life of the world
is His flesh. Then, in verses 53–56, He speaks of eating the flesh of the Son
of Man and drinking His blood. Consider the shock those words would
have caused among Jewish listeners. The Torah prohibits consuming
blood (Leviticus 17:10–14; Deuteronomy 12:23–25). Jesus was not
addressing people unfamiliar with the holiness of blood; He was speaking
to people formed by the Law and the prophets.

We should not erase the difficulty of the text. The Gospel itself records
that some of His disciples described the teaching as hard to accept (John
6:60), and many ceased following Him (John 6:66). Jesus then asked the
Twelve whether they also wished to leave. Simon Peter gave the
wonderful reply that has strengthened the Church through the centuries:
Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life (John 6:68).

What did Jesus mean? His discourse is about receiving the life that He
gives through His self-offering. Verse 63 also emphasizes the life-giving
Spirit and His words. Within the symbolic reading emphasized in this
sermon, Jesus was not commanding the physical consumption of human
flesh or literal drinking of human blood. Rather, He was directing hearers
to receive Him by faith and to depend on the sacrifice He would offer. [2]

Christian traditions debate the precise connection between John 6 and
sacramental theology, so we should not pretend that every church reads
the details in exactly the same way. But no responsible Christian
interpretation understands the Lord’s Supper as an instruction for
believers to carve flesh from Jesus’ body or to practice cannibalism. The
question is not whether Jesus spoke in vivid language—He certainly did.
The question is what His words meant in the setting of His saving mission.


3. The Objection: Dionysus, Bacchus and Ritual
Meals
Some critics argue that Christianity borrowed its sacred meal from the
religion of Dionysus. Dionysus was a Greek deity associated especially
with wine, festivity, theatre and ecstatic worship. In Roman religion he
was also known as Bacchus. Ancient writers and artists describe
remarkable traditions around his worship; these deserve careful study,
not dismissal. [3]

A worshipper who drinks wine, a god associated with life, a communal
meal, an animal sacrifice, and a description of consuming flesh: these
details may sound familiar to someone looking for parallels with Christian
language about the body and blood of Christ. But one vital question must
always be asked: do these similarities demonstrate that Jesus or the
apostles actually copied a Dionysian ritual?

In 1923, Khwaja Kamal-ud-Din published Sirāj-e-Hidāyat: Yanābīʿ al-Masīḥ
(‫ ینابیع المسیح‬:‫)سراِج ہدایت‬, issued by the Muslim Book Society in Lahore.
This is a verifiable historical publication—not merely a title recalled from
the sermon. A related English-language book, The Sources of Christianity,
was first published in 1924. Kamal-ud-Din argued that elements of
Christian doctrine and worship derived from older pagan religious
traditions. In this sermon, I address especially the allegation that Holy
Communion originated in the Dionysian cult. Although the publication
details are confirmed, a precise passage in the 1923 Urdu volume stating
the Dionysus–Communion comparison has not yet been established by
page-specific inspection. I therefore distinguish his general claim about
pagan influences from the exact argument as reported in my oral
sermon. The Christian responses associated with Archdeacon Barkat
Ullah, including Nūr al-Hudā, deserve separate investigation of the
original editions. [4]

My appeal to friends in the Ahmadiyya community, and to all who
question Christian faith, is simple: let us conduct the discussion with
respect and evidence. We need not insult one another to investigate
history. Let a critic present the original text and the page; let the Christian
answer with Scripture and responsible historical reasoning.


4. What the Ancient and Modern Sources Actually
Say
Euripides and The Bacchae
The most important ancient work in this discussion is The Bacchae, a
tragedy by the Greek playwright Euripides, written near the end of the
fifth century BCE and generally dated to its posthumous performance
around 405 BCE. The drama portrays Dionysus and his followers,
including women known as maenads or bacchants. It contains scenes of

ecstatic action, the tearing apart of animals (sparagmos), and a reference
to raw flesh (omophagia); see especially lines 135–139 and 734–747. [5]

Let us be precise with Greek terminology. Sparagmos means rending or
tearing apart. Omophagia means eating raw flesh. The two words are
related in discussions of Dionysian rites, but they are not synonyms. The
play also depicts the violent dismemberment of Pentheus (approximately
lines 1122–1142), an episode within a dramatic narrative.

Does the existence of this tragedy prove that every Dionysian gathering
practiced raw-meat consumption? No. Drama is evidence for ancient
religious ideas and literary representation, but its extraordinary scenes
cannot simply be treated as a photograph of ordinary worship
everywhere in Greece. Modern classical scholarship warns that the play’s
portrayals may intensify or stylize much milder ritual practices. Nor does
the play contain the Christian institution words from the Last Supper. [6]

Seven influential modern books
It is important to name the scholars correctly and to separate what they
studied from what later readers claimed. Andrew Lang (1844–1912)
published Myth, Ritual and Religion in 1887, examining myths and rituals
comparatively. William Robertson Smith (1846–1894) published Lectures
on the Religion of the Semites: First Series, The Fundamental Institutions
in 1889, studying ancient Semitic religious institutions and sacrifice. Both
works helped shape the broader scholarly conversation about ritual, but
neither title itself proves that Jesus adopted a Greek ceremony. [7]

Sir James George Frazer (1854–1941) first published The Golden Bough: A
Study in Magic and Religion in 1890. Its ambitious comparative framework
expanded over successive editions, including the twelve-volume third
edition issued in stages in the early twentieth century. Frazer explored
recurring patterns of sacrifice, vegetation, sacred kingship and religion.
Such comparisons influenced many later authors, but they must not be
mistaken for a documentary chain from Dionysus to the Christian Last
Supper. [8]

Gilbert Murray (1866–1957) was a classicist who edited and translated
Greek tragedy, including Euripides. His work made the plays available to
English readers and influenced ritual approaches to Greek drama. Jane
Ellen Harrison (1850–1928) published Themis: A Study of the Social Origins

of Greek Religion in 1912, with an excursus on ritual forms in Greek
tragedy by Murray. Their work investigated Greek religious symbolism; it
did not witness the Last Supper. [9]

Sigmund Freud (1856–1939) published Totem und Tabu (Totem and
Taboo) in 1913 after serial essays in 1912–1913. He advanced a
psychoanalytic theory of taboo, sacrifice and collective life. It is a modern
theory—not an ancient eyewitness report about Jesus. [10]

Edward Carpenter (1844–1929) published Pagan and Christian Creeds:
Their Origin and Meaning in 1920. Chapter 4, “Totem-Sacraments and
Eucharists,” is especially relevant. Carpenter explicitly compared the
Christian Eucharist to earlier sacred-meal and Dionysian material, drawing
on other writers and sources. This is a genuine comparative thesis and
should be represented fairly. Yet an author’s argument of derivation is not
the same as a demonstrated historical route of borrowing; the evidence
supporting that argument must still be tested. [11]


5. Similarity Is Not Proof of Borrowing
Here is the central historical principle of our investigation: resemblance
does not automatically establish dependence. Across societies, people
share meals, use bread and drink, celebrate harvests, and speak about
death and life. To prove direct borrowing, we would need more than a list
of common features. We would need a credible sequence of events; a
plausible channel of contact; evidence that the relevant Christian teachers
knew and adopted the particular ceremony; and distinctive connections
unlikely to arise independently.

Consider the contrast. The dramatic portrait of Dionysian ecstasy includes
dismemberment, frenzy and the tearing of animals. In the New
Testament, Jesus calmly takes bread, gives thanks, breaks it, and gives it
to His disciples. He then gives a cup and interprets His approaching death
in the language of covenant. Paul’s teaching calls the Church to examine
itself, recognize the body, avoid humiliation of poorer believers, and
proclaim the Lord’s death (1 Corinthians 10:16–21; 11:17–34). The content,
setting, and purpose need careful comparison, not superficial equation.

Early Christian testimony also exists within the first century: Paul wrote of
the Lord’s Supper in 1 Corinthians 11:23–26, generally dated to the middle

of that century. The Gospels describe Jesus’ final meal. Later Christian
witnesses, including the Didache (chapters 9–10 and 14) and Justin Martyr
(First Apology 65–67), show the development and understanding of
Christian thanksgiving and communal worship. These texts place the
Lord’s Supper in a Jewish and early Christian setting. [12]

This does not mean that all comparisons with Greek religion are
worthless. Serious comparison can illuminate the wider Mediterranean
world. But a careful historian will distinguish possible cultural parallels
from a claim of demonstrable origins. Our case for the Lord’s Supper
begins with the Scriptures themselves—especially Israel’s Passover, Jesus’
covenant language, and the apostolic witness.


6. The Passover Roots of the Lord’s Supper
Open your Bible to Exodus 12. Long before the written Christian Gospels,
Scripture tells how Israel, enslaved in Egypt, prepared for liberation. God
commanded each household to take an unblemished lamb, slaughter it,
mark the doorposts and lintel with its blood, and eat the meal with
unleavened bread and bitter herbs (Exodus 12:3–11). The blood was a
sign, and the households so marked were spared when judgment came
upon Egypt (Exodus 12:12–13).

Notice the pattern: a spotless lamb, blood associated with protection, a
shared meal, deliverance, and a command to remember. This became the
foundation of the biblical Passover, celebrated by Israel in remembrance
of the exodus (Exodus 12:14, 24–27). The Jewish festival is called Pesaḥ,
usually connected with passing over or protecting. The text gives its own
theological explanation; we do not need to invent a Greek origin for it.

Let us also speak responsibly about chronology. Exodus recounts an
event set in Israel’s ancient past, but scholars disagree about the dating
and historical reconstruction of the exodus, and independent
archaeological confirmation of every detail has not been established. The
Christian theological argument here is based primarily on the canonical
narrative and its later biblical reception, not on a claim that archaeological
debate is settled. [13]

In Exodus, Israel did not drink the lamb’s blood. The blood had a distinct
function, and the meal had another. Leviticus later prohibits consumption

of blood because the life is in the blood (Leviticus 17:11–14). This
difference matters when a critic imagines that the Christian institution
requires literal blood drinking. Jesus uses covenant and sacrificial imagery
already deeply established in the Hebrew Scriptures. Exodus 24:8 also
speaks of the blood of the covenant—language that echoes strongly in
the New Testament.


7. The Last Supper and the New Covenant
Now turn to Luke 22:15–20. Jesus tells His disciples of His earnest desire
to eat the Passover with them before He suffers. The Synoptic Gospels
explicitly place the meal in a Passover setting (Matthew 26:17–29; Mark
14:12–25; Luke 22:7–20). John’s Gospel presents a debated chronological
relationship to Passover (John 13:1; 18:28); this question has generated
different scholarly explanations and should not be concealed. [14]

At the table Jesus takes bread. He does not cut a piece of flesh from His
arm. He gives thanks, breaks bread and gives it to His disciples with
words identifying it with His body given for them. Here we see the heart
of the sermon’s symbolic understanding: the bread points to the body He
would offer, and believers receive it in remembrance of Him (Luke 22:19).

After supper He takes the cup and speaks of the new covenant in His
blood, poured out for His people (Luke 22:20). In Matthew 26:28, His
blood is connected with forgiveness of sins. The background is not merely
a generic religious meal but the story of Israel’s deliverance and the
prophetic hope of a new covenant (Jeremiah 31:31–34). At Calvary the
promised saving work comes to its decisive expression.

What about the contents of the cup? Jesus calls the drink “fruit of the vine”
(Luke 22:18; Matthew 26:29). The text does not specify a modern
laboratory measure of alcohol content. Some Christians use unfermented
grape juice; others use wine. I affirm in this sermon that the Communion
celebration must never be an excuse for drunkenness, a point fully
consistent with the New Testament’s opposition to drunkenness
(Ephesians 5:18; 1 Corinthians 11:21). But it would go beyond the quoted
verses to insist that “fruit of the vine” definitively proves an unfermented
drink.

Holy Communion is therefore not an invitation to intoxication, frenzy or
loss of self-control. It is a solemn and joyful act of thanksgiving,
remembrance and discipleship. The symbols direct our hearts to Christ’s
sacrifice. The Lord’s Supper should lead to love, holiness, gratitude and
renewed obedience.


8. Christ Our Passover Lamb
Beloved Church, now consider the words of John the Baptist: “Behold, the
Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!” (John 1:29). The
Passover story does not end in Egypt. It finds a profound Christian
fulfillment in Jesus Christ. Paul writes that Christ, our Passover, has been
sacrificed (1 Corinthians 5:7). Peter says believers were redeemed not with
silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb
without blemish or spot (1 Peter 1:18–19).

The lamb in Exodus was without defect; the New Testament testifies to
Christ’s sinlessness (Hebrews 4:15; 1 Peter 2:22). The Passover speaks of
deliverance; the cross speaks of redemption from sin. The ancient meal
was a remembrance of liberation; Communion is a proclamation of the
saving death of Jesus, the risen Lord. This is not a claim that the two rites
are identical in every detail. It is the biblical typology through which
Christians understand Jesus’ sacrifice.

Jesus even challenges His opponents with the question, “Which one of you
convicts me of sin?” (John 8:46). His character, His obedience, His self-
giving love, and His death on the cross reveal why the apostles identify
Him as the spotless Lamb. In Him we see not the destruction of a victim
during religious frenzy, but the willing offering of the Son for the life of
the world.

When I say there is power in the blood of Jesus, I am confessing what His
death accomplished: atonement, reconciliation, forgiveness, and the hope
of eternal life. We do not put literal blood upon our doors today; we trust
the crucified and risen Christ. His sacrifice is sufficient. Our response is
faith, repentance and obedience.

9. Proclaiming His Death Until He Comes
Let us return to 1 Corinthians 11:23–26. Paul reminds the congregation
that on the night Jesus was betrayed, He took bread, gave thanks, broke
it, and spoke of His body. In the same way, after supper He took the cup
and spoke of the new covenant in His blood. Then Paul explains the
significance: whenever believers eat this bread and drink this cup, they
proclaim the Lord’s death until He comes.

What are we declaring when we celebrate Holy Communion? We declare
that Jesus died for our sins, that He was buried, that God raised Him from
the dead, and that He is coming again (1 Corinthians 15:3–4; 11:26). We do
not celebrate death as a defeat. We proclaim the death that became the
means of our salvation, and we announce the hope of the resurrection.

But hear Paul’s warning as well. The Corinthians had turned their
gathering into a place of division and inequality. Some went hungry while
others ate excessively (1 Corinthians 11:20–22). So Paul called them to
examine themselves, discern the significance of what they were doing,
and care for one another (11:27–34). Holy Communion is not merely an
argument to win against critics. It must transform our relationships inside
the Church.

Therefore, let every believer approach the Lord’s Table with reverence,
with gratitude, and with genuine faith. Let those who are divided seek
reconciliation. Let those carrying burdens seek the Lord’s mercy. Let the
rich remember the poor, and let the whole congregation bear witness
that we belong to one Lord. The table points beyond itself to the Saviour
who gave Himself for us.


10. Invitation, Prayer and Ministry Appeal
I now invite every person listening in the sanctuary or watching from afar
to consider the words of Jesus Christ. God so loved the world that He gave
His only Son, so that everyone who believes in Him should not perish but
have eternal life (John 3:16). John writes that whoever has the Son has life
(1 John 5:11–12). Dear friend, do you have this life?

To our Muslim and Ahmadi neighbours, I extend this invitation with
respect. The Qur’an also contains the account of a table spread requested

by the disciples of Jesus (Sūrat al-Mā’idah 5:112–115). That passage is not
itself the biblical institution narrative of Holy Communion, and the two
accounts should not be declared identical. But it may serve as a starting
point for respectful conversation about Jesus, the disciples, the divine
provision of food, and the meaning of faith. [15]

Let us celebrate the Lord’s Table with understanding. When we take the
bread, we remember His body given for us. When we take the cup, we
remember His blood shed for us. We proclaim His death, confess His
resurrection, and await His coming. Nothing in this celebration requires
the worship of Dionysus, Bacchus, or any other deity. Our worship
belongs to the one true God revealed in Jesus Christ.

Now, would you bow your head? If there is sin in your life, ask the Lord for
forgiveness. If you are sick, discouraged, anxious, grieving, or weighed
down by difficulties, turn toward Christ in faith. We do not promise that
every difficulty disappears instantly; we bring every need before our
gracious Father. The Lord hears the prayer of His people.

Heavenly Father, in the name of Jesus Christ, I thank You for Your Holy
Word and for the great gift of salvation. We confess that Jesus Christ, our
Passover Lamb, gave Himself for our redemption. Forgive our sins and
cleanse our hearts. Strengthen those who are weak, comfort those who
mourn, grant courage to those who suffer, and bring healing according to
Your mercy and wisdom. Let each person who hears this message know
the life and hope found in Your Son. Draw us into unity, holiness and
compassionate service. We ask these things in the mighty name of our
Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ. Amen.
